Predicting Extra Paid Mathematics Classes from Student Background

Author

Vikasini Senthilkumar, Shorya Jain, Chengyan(Yan) Zhao, Arunark Singh

Summary

We looked at whether students’ family backgrounds and study habits could help predict who attended paid mathematics classes. Using data from 395 students at two Portuguese schools, we compared three models. Logistic regression performed best during cross-validation, with an average accuracy of 64.2%. On the test data, its accuracy was 58.6%, slightly higher than the 54.5% accuracy of always predicting the most common outcome. This suggests the information in our dataset offers some help with prediction, but the results are modest. The data covers only two schools, and the model doesn’t include every possible reason a student might attend tutoring. It also shows patterns in the data, not that a student’s background or study habits cause them to take paid classes.

Introduction

Private tutoring provides students with additional academic instruction outside regular school lessons. This includes help with coursework and examination preparation. In their study of tutoring agencies in Ontario, Aurini and Davies (2004) describe how private tutoring expanded from informal assistance into organized learning centres that offer a broader range of educational services. This suggests that paid tuition is becoming a trend. However, we believe that participation in paid tutoring may reflect more than academic difficulty. Using German household survey data, Hille, Spieß, and Staneva (2016) document differences in tutoring participation across income groups. They highlighted the importance of considering family resources when studying access to supplementary education. In Portugal, Fonseca (1996) examines how family resources and competition for educational qualifications shape educational opportunities and strategies. Although this research concerns higher education rather than mathematics tutoring specifically, it still provides historical context for examining family background in educational participation. More directly, Qu (2025) analyzes mathematics achievement among students in two Portuguese secondary schools. This research considered family, individual, and school-related characteristics, including paid tutoring. Building on these perspectives, our study aims to understand which student characteristics help distinguish participants from non-participants. This may inform further research into access to supplementary learning opportunities. We ask: Can students’ family backgrounds and study-related characteristics predict whether they attend paid tutoring in mathematics? To answer this question, we use the mathematics subset of the Student Performance dataset from the UCI Machine Learning Repository (Cortez 2008). It contains 395 student records from two Portuguese secondary schools, collected through school reports and questionnaires, where each row represents one student’s record for the mathematics course. Our predictors include parents’ education levels and occupations, family size, whether the parents live together, weekly study time, past class failures, family educational support, the school the student attends, and whether the student lives in an urban or rural area. The response variable is paid mathematics tutoring participation, recorded as “yes” or “no”, which makes this a binary classification problem. We exclude first-period, second-period, and final grades because these may already reflect tutoring participation. Our analysis evaluates predictive relationships within this sample. It does not establish why students attend tutoring or whether tutoring improves their grades.

We downloaded the student performance data set directly from the UCI Machine Learning Repository using code, so that anyone running this report gets the same data. The download is a zip file that contains a second zip file, so we unzipped both and saved the original files in the raw data folder. Data cleaning

To prepare the data for analysis, we kept only the response variable and the 11 predictors related to our question. We gave each variable a clearer name and converted all text variables into categories with “yes” as the first level of the response variable. The data had no missing values or duplicate rows.

Several variables were stored as short codes, so we replaced them with readable labels.

  • Home address is now “urban” and “rural” instead of “U” and “R”.

  • Parents’ living situation is “together” and “apart” instead of “T” and “A”.

  • Family size is “3 or fewer” and “greater than 3” instead of “LE3” and “GT3”.

  • The two schools are shown by their full names.

  • Weekly study time was recorded as a number from 1 to 4, so we replaced each number with the time range it stands for, i.e. less than 2 hours, 2 to 5 hours, 5 to 10 hours, and more than 10 hours.

We saved the cleaned data to the processed data folder.

Splitting the data

Before exploring the data, we split it into a training set of 75% with 296 students and a test set of 25% with 99 students. We used a stratified split so that both sets have a similar share of students who take paid classes. We also fixed the random seed so the split is the same every time the report runs. All exploratory analysis and model building use only the training set, and the test set is kept aside for the final evaluation.

import pandas as pd
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
math_student = pd.read_csv("../data/processed/student_math_clean.csv")

# Splitting the data into 75% training and 25% test
train_df, test_df = train_test_split(math_student, test_size = 0.25, stratify=math_student["paid_classes"], random_state = 522)

Classification Model

We compare three classifiers using five-fold stratified cross-validation on the training data. The model with the highest mean validation accuracy is selected. We then fit that model on the full training set and report its performance once on the held-out test set. The test set is not used to choose a model.

We use logistic regression as a simple linear baseline, a shallow decision tree for an interpretable rule-based model, and a small random forest to check whether combining trees improves prediction. The categorical predictors are one-hot encoded, and numeric predictors are standardized within each training fold. The same pre-processing is applied consistently to all three models.

Cross-validation model fitting

import numpy as np
import altair as alt
from IPython.display import display
from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.preprocessing import OneHotEncoder, StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedKFold, cross_val_score
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score, precision_score, recall_score, f1_score, confusion_matrix

# These are the selected predictors; the response is paid_classes.
categorical_columns = [
    "mother_job", "father_job", "family_size", "parent_status",
    "studytime", "family_support", "school", "address"
]
numeric_columns = ["mother_edu", "father_edu", "past_failures"]
predictor_columns = categorical_columns + numeric_columns

X_train = train_df[predictor_columns]
y_train = train_df["paid_classes"].astype(str)
X_test = test_df[predictor_columns]
y_test = test_df["paid_classes"].astype(str)

preprocessor = ColumnTransformer(
    transformers=[
        ("numeric", StandardScaler(), numeric_columns),
        ("categorical", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"), categorical_columns),
    ]
)

model_choices = {
    "Logistic regression": LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000, random_state=522),
    "Decision tree (depth 4)": DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=4, random_state=522),
    "Random forest": RandomForestClassifier(
        n_estimators=100, max_depth=5, random_state=522, n_jobs=-1
    ),
}

cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=522)
model_pipelines = {}
cv_rows = []

for model_name, classifier in model_choices.items():
    pipeline = Pipeline([
        ("preprocess", preprocessor),
        ("classifier", classifier),
    ])
    scores = cross_val_score(
        pipeline, X_train, y_train, cv=cv, scoring="accuracy"
    )
    model_pipelines[model_name] = pipeline
    cv_rows.append({
        "Model": model_name,
        "Mean validation accuracy": scores.mean(),
        "SD across folds": scores.std(),
    })

cv_results = pd.DataFrame(cv_rows).sort_values(
    "Mean validation accuracy", ascending=False
).reset_index(drop=True)

solver solver: {'lbfgs', 'liblinear', 'newton-cg', 'newton-cholesky', 'sag', 'saga'}, default='lbfgs'

Algorithm to use in the optimization problem. Default is 'lbfgs'.
To choose a solver, you might want to consider the following aspects:

- 'lbfgs' is a good default solver because it works reasonably well for a wide
class of problems.
- For :term:`multiclass` problems (`n_classes >= 3`), all solvers except
'liblinear' minimize the full multinomial loss, 'liblinear' will raise an
error.
- 'newton-cholesky' is a good choice for
`n_samples` >> `n_features * n_classes`, especially with one-hot encoded
categorical features with rare categories. Be aware that the memory usage
of this solver has a quadratic dependency on `n_features * n_classes`
because it explicitly computes the full Hessian matrix.
- For small datasets, 'liblinear' is a good choice, whereas 'sag'
and 'saga' are faster for large ones;
- 'liblinear' can only handle binary classification by default. To apply a
one-versus-rest scheme for the multiclass setting one can wrap it with the

The five-fold cross-validation comparison selected logistic regression, with a mean validation accuracy of 64.2% (standard deviation 2.4 percentage points). On the held-out test set, it achieved 58.6% accuracy, compared with 54.5% for the majority-class baseline. For students who attended paid classes, precision was 53.8%, recall was 62.2%, and F1 was 0.577. The confusion matrix shows 28 correctly identified students who attended classes, 30 correctly identified students who did not, 24 false positives, and 17 false negatives. Discussion

The results provide a limited answer to our research question. Logistic regression performed best in cross-validation, but its 58.6% test accuracy was only slightly higher than the 54.5% majority-class baseline. It correctly identified 28 of the 45 students who attended paid mathematics classes and 30 of the 54 who did not. However, it also predicted tutoring for 24 students who did not attend, and missed 17 who did. So, while the selected characteristics may help distinguish between the two groups, they do not give a reliable prediction for an individual student.

We expected family and study-related characteristics to provide some useful information, since decisions about extra classes may be connected to students’ learning experiences and family circumstances. But this dataset cannot tell us why a student attended tutoring, and our model does not include factors such as family income, academic goals, or a student’s perceived need for extra help. The data also cover only 395 students from two schools in Portugal, so the results may not apply to students in other schools or countries. Finally, these are associations used for prediction; they do not show that a particular background or study habit causes a student to attend paid classes.

A useful next step would be to repeat the analysis with students from more schools and include measures of tutoring access, family resources, and reasons for seeking extra help. That could help determine whether the modest performance here reflects limits in the predictors, the small sample, or both.
